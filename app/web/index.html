Replacing the Streamlit dashboard

An instrument,
not a dashboard.

The pipeline turns a multi-hour reservoir simulation into a sub-second screening pass. A frontend that renders that as static matplotlib PNGs throws the whole point away. This is the argument for a client-side instrument: the field animates, the fault ensemble is spatial, and every number wears its provenance.

The accents are not chosen for taste. They are sampled from viridis and inferno — the two colormaps app/dashboard.py already uses for saturation and pressure. A legend colour and a UI colour meaning the same thing is the cheapest kind of coherence.

Why the weight toggle is the point

The three weights are ASSUMED, and re-scoring under different weightings moves the top ten by 2–5 seats. A static leaderboard hides that. A toggle that visibly reshuffles the ranking in front of the audience makes the caveat impossible to miss — and turns a weakness in the method into the most memorable interaction on the page.

Tier bands, not ranks, are what survive the sweep. Say “this site is in the top decile,” never “site 468 is the best site.”

The fault swarm replaces the histogram

Today the Monte-Carlo ensemble is a histogram of 25 probabilities — an abstraction of something inherently spatial. Draw the sampled faults where they are, coloured by risk, and the physics reads itself: the dangerous ones sit on the plume front, near the pressure ridge. Hover any segment on the slab above. The worst-case fault is extruded up through the caprock, because that vertical path is the leak.

The fault is not in the simulation. It is overlaid on the predicted fields — which is exactly what makes a live sweep of unknown fault properties affordable in the browser.

04 / BUILD

How this ships without a backend

The 12.38 GB dataset is not on the laptop and the disk is nearly full, so a live-inference backend is the wrong shape. Everything above runs client-side, which also means it deploys as a static site and demos with the Wi-Fi off.

01

Ship a demo pack, not a dataset

Export ~24 test-set simulations from the Kaggle kernel — where the data already lives — as quantised uint8 sprite sheets of pressure and saturation. 24 sims × 60 steps × 128² × 2 fields is trivially small once PNG-compressed, and that is the entire field payload the frontend ever needs.

02

Run XGBoost in the browser

Dump the booster to JSON and walk the trees in ~150 lines of TypeScript. Then the fault Monte-Carlo is interactive: drag a fault, re-sample the ensemble, watch probabilities move at frame rate. That interaction is impossible against a Python round-trip and it is the single strongest demo moment available.

03

Port the Darcy flux to JS

fault_leakage_flux is closed-form. Reimplemented in JS it is a handful of lines, and it keeps the physics — not just the ML — live under the user's hands. Guard it with the same monotonicity self-checks src/leakage/labels.py already runs.

04

Keep the U-Net optional

The surrogate is the one piece that genuinely wants a GPU. Precompute its predictions into the same demo pack and expose the simulator-vs-surrogate toggle the Streamlit app already has — the 99.0% PR-AUC retention is a result worth showing, not hiding.

05

Vite + TypeScript, canvas over chart libraries

Every visual here is a canvas and a few hundred lines of arithmetic. A charting library would fight the projection, the colormaps and the ribbon, and would still not draw the slab. Reach for one only if a plain line chart appears — and it probably will not.

Status of the data on this page

REAL — the 1,000-site atlas, its scores, ranks and criteria, read from outputs/site_suitability_ranking.csv.

MOCKUP — the reservoir fields, the fault ensemble and the attribution bars. The source dataset is not on this machine, so these are procedural stand-ins that demonstrate the visual language and the interaction, not model output. Nothing on this page should be quoted as a result.
